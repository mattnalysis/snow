## Case caption

**State of Ohio, ex rel. Dave Yost, Attorney General** (Plaintiff, filed at ODNR's request, June 2020) **v. The Landings Property Owners Association, Inc., et al.** — Knox Co. C.P. No. 20IN06-0149.

Defendants / third-party defendants: The Landings Property Owners Association, Inc.; Mullins Bros. Ltd. (developer); the City of Mount Vernon; and the ~68-105 Phase VII/VIII lot owners (brought in later by Mullins Bros.' third-party complaint), collectively referred to in filings as **"the Long Defendants"** (after group liaison Mark A. Long) — includes Matthew M. Snow and Megan Snow.

## What "open and pending" means here

The case has not been closed by final judgment, settlement-with-dismissal, or individual dismissal orders. Every named party remains legally bound by it. It can sit quiet for long stretches (as it has) without ceasing to be "pending" — the only ways out are a final judgment resolving every remaining claim, or a dismissal order for each party.

## What's left for the court to decide

The Sept. 23, 2024 ruling decided dam ownership (POA owns it, not Mullins, not individual homeowners) and held the Phase VII/VIII owners bound by the recorded Declarations. It explicitly **reserved for trial**, with no trial date set:
- Indemnity and contribution (cross-claims)
- Injunctive relief
- Water Pollution Control Act violations
- Breach of the Declarations/contract

Also still pending: the Receiver's motion to approve the ~$45,000 one-time vegetation assessment and the monthly maintenance assessment, and — the actual goal everyone's working toward — dismissal of the individual homeowners, which has not happened yet.

## The Long Defendants' own claims (not just defenses)

They didn't only defend — they filed affirmative claims of their own:

**Counterclaim against the State** — Count One sought a declaratory judgment that the Long Defendants are **not** owners of the dam. This is the mirror image of the State's own Count One (declaring who *is* the owner). **Status: effectively decided in the homeowners' favor** by the Sept. 2024 ruling.

**Cross-Claim against Mullins Bros. Ltd.** — per the Long Defendants' own Motion for Default Judgment against Mullins Bros. (filed 2023-07-25, source: Gmail thread `1898f365ee55c3fb`, doc `Long Defendants Motion for Default Judgment Against Mullins Bros. Ltd..pdf`):

> "On June 2, 2023, the Long Defendants served their Cross-claims... The Long Defendants asserted **a five-count Cross-claim** against Mullins Bros., Ltd., including claims for **declaratory judgment, breach of contract, and indemnity or contribution**." (Amended June 9, 2023.)

The Long Defendants later moved for summary judgment on **Counts I, II, and IV** of that cross-claim (source: `Reply Memo ISO Motion for Summary Judgment as to Mullins Bros. Ltd. 7239769.1.pdf`), arguing they're entitled to affirmative relief because none of them own the dam.

- Mullins Bros. initially **defaulted** on answering the cross-claim (missed the July 7, 2023 deadline, prompting the default-judgment motion above), but later got leave to answer out of time and filed its own competing summary-judgment motion arguing it hasn't owned the property since the 2007 transfer to the POA.
- **Status of Counts I, II, IV** (declaratory judgment / breach of contract): effectively resolved via the same Sept. 2024 ownership ruling.
- **Status of indemnity/contribution** (the remaining count, not among I/II/IV): explicitly **still open**, reserved for trial per the court's own language. Mullins argued this claim "fails because Mullins Bros. has no liability" — the court did not rule on that; it remains outstanding.

## How the Receiver's fee is being paid

Not by the homeowners directly, and not out of the ARPA repair funds. Per the Interim Order appointing Jack Harris (Intercept Management Corp.) as receiver (Oct. 3-4, 2024):

> "Any funds that are expended by or on behalf of the receiver, including receivership fees, fees for professionals assisting the receivership... **shall be taxed as court costs or otherwise treated as an administrative expense** of the action."

And specifically, the order directed:

> "the City shall set aside immediately the sum of **$70,000.00** to be reserved for fees and expenses of the receiver, to be released from time to time upon application of the receiver and pursuant to orders of this Court."

So: the City of Mount Vernon set aside a **$70,000 reserve fund** at the outset; the Receiver draws against it periodically by filing fee applications, which the court has to approve before funds are released. This is legally treated as a court cost / administrative expense of the litigation, not a separate bill to the Phase VII/VIII owners.

The Receiver's **first fee application** was filed with his **Fourth Interim Report** (2025-05-28, source: filing by Strip Hoppers/receiver's counsel via Eastman & Smith, doc id `1971905d8a1d62fe`). Approving further/ongoing fee applications remains one of the open "administrative tail" items (see `STATUS.md`).

## Why ownership (narrow) and maintenance cost-sharing (broad) don't line up (2026-09-23)

Matt's question — if the Landings POA (bound only by Phase VII/VIII's Declarations) is the legal owner, why are more neighborhoods being asked to pay maintenance? — sent me back into the record, and the honest answer is that **the case never reconciled the two**.

**Ownership/liability is narrow and court-ordered:** the Sept. 2024 ruling binds only the ~68 Phase VII/VIII lot owners, because only they were sued and only they are subject to the recorded Declarations that make them POA members. Waters Edge sits outside this because it has its own separate, pre-existing HOA. Mallard Pointe, Coventry Court, and Woodbury sit outside it because nobody has ever sued them or bound them to any Declaration.

**The ongoing maintenance fee is broad and was never litigated.** The ~$36,000/year (245 properties, $147/property/year, $12.23/property/month) comes from a document titled **"Basin 01 (Pond 1A) Utility Fee Model"** (referenced via Stephen Chappelear's Aug. 13, 2026 email, doc `KCCD Summary 20260904.pdf`/related engineering summary) — a stormwater-drainage-basin cost allocation, not an ownership-based one. It's being proposed as a City utility-bill line item covering everyone whose property drains into the pond, independent of who's a lawsuit party or a POA member.

**The gap between the two is not hypothetical — the court flagged it once and it was never followed up on.** The Long Defendants' own interrogatory responses (document `18b2baa900ca06a2`, Long Defendants' Responses to the State's Interrogatories, answer to Interrogatory ~21) state, on information and belief: a **July 26, 2022 Journal Entry** ordered Mullins Bros. to identify, from a list to be provided by the City, "any property owner in the watershed basin... which contribute water flow to the lake and the Dam" for possible joinder as necessary parties under Civ. R. 19 and 20. **"Those property owners have not been joined in this case."** In other words, the court itself once floated the idea that the broader watershed-contributing properties might need to be legally bound — and then it never happened.

**This is exactly why Receiver Jack Harris is using consent letters, not court orders, for everyone outside Phase VII/VIII.** The June 21, 2026 "Dam Maintenance Letter — do not sign" episode (thread `19ee815e25505ce1`) makes this explicit: Harris circulated a signature letter approving future maintenance costs that "later emerges... went to litigation-group members in error — intended only for non-HOA homeowners in the watershed." Phase VII/VIII owners don't need to consent to anything — a court order already binds them. Everyone else does, because nothing does.

**Even the group's own engineering summary flags the allocation as unresolved**, listing as open questions: "Why 245 units are included in the cost allocation?", "Does every property receive equal benefit?", "Should condos pay the same as single-family homes?" — the same tension Chappelear separately raised about the vegetation assessment (should lakefront owners pay more?). Nobody — not the court, not the receiver, not the group itself — has actually reconciled the ownership theory with the payment theory. **Correction to prior figures:** the parcel count should read **245**, not "~200," and the per-property costs are **$147/year / $12.23/month** per this model (the earlier "$14-19/month" figure appears to be a different, less refined estimate from an earlier point in the negotiation — worth treating the $12.23/mo figure as the more current one, sourced to the Aug. 13, 2026 utility-fee model specifically).

### A. Dam repair — the three permanent alternatives (Receiver's Sixth Interim Report, filed ~July 24, 2025)

Per the report itself: "In the Fifth Interim Report of Receiver, the Receiver detailed three alternatives to permanently cure the condition of the existing dam."

| Alternative | Cost | Description | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — maintain Class I structure | **$1,099,750.00** | Keeps the dam under full ODNR regulation; "by far the most expensive," would need long-term financing beyond ARPA funds, debt service falling on "the residents and the Property Owners Association in some form or fashion" | **Rejected** |
| 2 — de-regulate, single pool ("Single-Pond") | **$303,525.00** | Reduces height/storage below 15 acre-feet, single pool, lowered floor | **Rejected** despite being cheapest to build — "ongoing maintenance costs would be greater... mostly associated with mowing and care for what would often be a marshy area" |
| 3 — two pools + causeway ("Two-Pond") | **$701,525.00** initial estimate | Lower pool confined by the dam embankment, upper pool by new causeway; "ongoing maintenance costs would be less than the other two alternatives" | **Selected** (court approval Nov. 12, 2025) |

The Long Defendants (per their Oct. 31, 2025 memorandum contra) preferred Alternative 2 (Single-Pond) specifically for its lower construction cost, warning of up to $200,000 in change-order risk on the more expensive Two-Pond option. The court sided with the receiver's engineering judgment (AK Hydro's flood-risk opinion) over the cost objection.

**Actual/adjusted Two-Pond cost, per the Receiver's later motion:** $635,200.00 — "$66,325 lower than the amount the Receiver had said was within the ARPA funds available" ($701,525 − $66,325 = $635,200, confirming these are the same figure at different drafting stages). Construction (Haynes Construction) finished substantially complete by August 2026, **paid entirely from the county's ARPA award** — no construction shortfall materialized, so the Park National Bank loan backstop (seven pond-frontage owners) was never drawn on.

**Earliest estimate on record:** a July 23, 2019 Knox Pages article — before the lawsuit was even filed (June 2020) — reported **$300,000** in dam repairs, with four Mount Vernon council members (themselves affected property owners) recused from deliberating due to conflict of interest.

**Interim risk-reduction measures (IRRM), separate from the permanent repair:** AK Hydro was hired for roughly **$200,000** (about half of Stantec's rejected bid, reported as ~$400,000), paid by the **City** using already-encumbered ARPA funds. A companion construction-funding note (source document unclear — worth another pass if it matters) references **$634,500** encumbered to Haynes Construction, an estimated **$350,000** cost for an unspecified phase, and a resulting **$284,500** balance of encumbered ARPA funds — these three figures are internally consistent ($634,500 − $350,000 = $284,500) but their exact relationship to the IRRM vs. the permanent Two-Pond work isn't fully resolved in what's been read so far.

## "Did we ever get answers for these?" — the March 26, 2024 Supplemental Memorandum (2026-09-22)

Matt flagged a Google Doc (`1IOoS2gEiBhHPvQwrj2apdttjBWj831AOr9rMBzbNkT0`) and asked whether its questions were ever answered. It's the Long Defendants' **Supplemental Memorandum Regarding Motions for Summary Judgment**, filed March 26, 2024, responding to five questions Judge Kimbler posed in a Feb. 23, 2024 journal entry, plus a sixth question the Long Defendants proposed themselves. This had not previously been logged in this research file. Its content is directly relevant to the ownership ruling and to entity #2/#3 confusion above.

**The five court questions and the Long Defendants' answers, as filed:**
1. **What happens to land owned by a nonprofit whose Articles are cancelled for failure to file?** It stays the corporation's property — R.C. 1702.35 vests title absolutely, and neither cancellation (R.C. 1702.59) nor the post-cancellation director-powers statute (R.C. 1702.49) forfeits it, per *N. Kenova Dev. Co. v. Wilson* (4th Dist. 2008). The Landings POA, Inc.'s articles were cancelled **Feb. 19, 2015** (per Mullins' own RFA admission — note this is a different date than exhibit item 25's "March 2, 2015"; both trace to the same SOS cancellation, worth reconciling if it ever matters) but it kept title to the dam parcel.
2. **Was a formal winding-up action (R.C. 1702.50) ever filed in Knox County?** No.
3. **Who does the County Auditor list as owner?** The Landings Property Owners Association, Inc., per the Aug. 1, 2007 quitclaim deed (Book 1091, Pages 637–639).
4. **Could any party now file a winding-up action?** No — no party has standing (the "corporation" has never been served, has no counsel, and — critically — **has never had any directors or members at all**: none were named in the Articles, no annual meeting was ever held, no bylaws were ever prepared, Steve Mullins under oath could not name a single person who ever served as a trustee, and 91 Long Defendant affidavits attest none of them was ever a director or member or ever received the Articles) — and Knox County has no jurisdiction because the "corporation" has no Knox County principal office at all.
5. **Could a court order title transferred elsewhere via that statute?** No — R.C. 1702.50(A)'s enumerated powers don't include ordering a transfer to a government body or another nonprofit.
6. **(Long Defendants' own added question) Can the 2007 deed be set aside because Mullins never properly formed/ran the corporation?** Their argument: yes — Mullins filed the Articles March 31, 2005, then quitclaimed the dam parcel to it over two years later (2007) for no consideration, without ever appointing directors, adopting bylaws, admitting members, keeping corporate books, filing tax returns, recording bylaws with the County Recorder as R.C. 5312.02(D) required by March 9, 2011, or notifying ODNR of the ownership change before transfer as R.C. 1521.062(K) required. Steve Mullins' own deposition (pp. 229–294) describes him personally running the "corporation" — he set up its bank account at his own bank, sent invoices, deposited checks, and paid costs without any homeowner authorization — "treating the corporation and its bank account as his personal piggy bank." Their conclusion: if that's right, **Mullins Bros. — not the POA and not any homeowner — is the true owner of the dam parcel**, the opposite of Mullins' own litigation position.

**Did the court answer these?** Yes, by necessary implication, in the **Sept. 23, 2024 Decision & Entry** (see "Key ruling" above) — but not by adopting the Long Defendants' Q6 theory. The court: held the (still-cancelled, never-organized) Landings POA, Inc. owns the dam anyway; appointed a receiver to act for it rather than pursuing the R.C. 1702.50 winding-up path Q2/Q4/Q5 concluded was unavailable; and did not set the 2007 deed aside despite the corporate-formalities failures documented in Q6. In other words, the court effectively agreed the corporation was never properly organized and had no functioning directors/members/bylaws — which is exactly why a receiver had to be appointed to act for it — but rejected the conclusion the Long Defendants drew from that fact (that the deed to it should be voided). No exhibit or transcript recovered so far records the court's own reasoning for rejecting Q6 specifically; only the ultimate ruling is in hand.
